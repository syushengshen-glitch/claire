Best AI tools for teachers: lesson planning, explanation, visuals, and accessible media

Use AI to reduce preparation time while protecting student privacy, academic integrity, and teacher judgment.

Reviewed October 1, 2026 · Research-based editorial guide · Advertising is labeled separately

Teachers use AI to save preparation time, create alternative explanations, and adapt materials for different learners. The highest-risk uses involve student data, grading, discipline, and confidential school information.

A safe classroom workflow starts with the school policy and the learning objective. AI can generate options, but the teacher remains responsible for accuracy, accessibility, age appropriateness, bias, and the final assessment decision.

Decision rule: keep a tool only when it improves the finished result after corrections. A fast first draft is not a win if it creates more review work.

How to choose

Prepare clearer learning materials without automating assessment or exposing student information. Start with one measurable job and use the same test material across every candidate.

  • Follow school, district, and data-protection rules before entering any student information.
  • Keep the learning objective and curriculum standard visible in every prompt.
  • Review generated questions for accuracy, bias, difficulty, and unintended clues.
  • Provide accessible formats and do not rely on AI captions without human review.
  • Never let an AI tool make a high-impact grading or discipline decision without teacher review.

The shortlist

1. ChatGPT

Use it to brainstorm lesson activities, differentiate examples, and draft quiz questions, then review difficulty, bias, accuracy, and alignment with the curriculum.

Relevant strength: Handles text, images, files, and voice in one workspace. Limit to check: Answers still need fact-checking.

Read the full ChatGPT evaluation

2. Claude

Claude can help summarize long public documents and reorganize lesson notes. Do not upload student records or private school material without approved safeguards.

Relevant strength: Strong long-context document work. Limit to check: Product availability can vary by region.

Read the full Claude evaluation

3. Canva Magic Studio

Canva helps create worksheets, slides, and visual explanations quickly. Check image rights and make sure visual design does not reduce readability for learners who need accessible formats.

Relevant strength: Easy for non-designers. Limit to check: Advanced AI features may require Pro.

Read the full Canva Magic Studio evaluation

4. Descript

Descript can turn recorded lessons or interviews into transcript-based edits and captions. Review captions carefully and follow school policy on recording students.

Relevant strength: Edit video by editing transcript. Limit to check: Not ideal for complex visual effects.

Read the full Descript evaluation

5. Otter.ai

Otter can support teacher meetings and planning notes, but student or parent conversations require consent, retention, and access controls before recording.

Relevant strength: Automatic transcription. Limit to check: Free minutes are limited.

A workflow that stays under control

  1. Write the learning objective, audience, and accessibility needs first.
  2. Use AI to generate activity options, examples, or explanation variants.
  3. Check subject accuracy and remove content that does not fit the curriculum.
  4. Review language, reading level, bias, and accessibility.
  5. Collect student work under the school's normal privacy and assessment rules.

Verdict

AI can be a useful preparation assistant for teachers when it remains inside approved privacy boundaries and supports teacher-designed learning. It should not become an unsupervised grader or a shortcut around student data rules.

Frequently asked questions

Can teachers upload student work to an AI tool?

Only if the school permits that tool and the data terms are approved. Many student records and assignments are confidential or regulated.

Should AI grade assignments?

AI may assist with feedback drafts or rubric checks, but a teacher should review high-impact grades and any decision that affects a student's record.

How can teachers use AI responsibly?

Protect student data, verify content, cite sources, follow curriculum and accessibility requirements, and keep the teacher responsible for the final decision.
