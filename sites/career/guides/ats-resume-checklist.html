ATS resume checklist: 14 checks before you apply

A repeatable pre-submission check that does not depend on chasing a perfect ATS score.

By CareerSignal Lab Editorial Team · Reviewed November 13, 2026 · 10 min read · Advertising is labeled separately

ATS advice becomes unhelpful when it turns into a hunt for a perfect score. Applicant tracking systems differ, employers configure them differently, and a human still makes most hiring decisions. The useful goal is simpler: make the resume easy to parse, easy to scan and easy to defend.

What an ATS can and cannot decide

An ATS may store an application, extract text, search for keywords, route a candidate or support a recruiter's review. It does not know whether you are a good colleague, whether your evidence is truthful, or whether a particular phrasing will impress every employer. A clean resume improves the chance that your information survives the trip from upload to review. It does not guarantee an interview.

Use this checklist as a quality gate. If a change makes the resume less accurate or less readable just to satisfy a score, do not make it.

The 14-point pre-submission checklist

  1. One target role per version. The headline and summary should match the role family without pretending to be a different profession.
  2. Standard section names. Use recognizable labels such as Summary, Experience, Education and Skills. Creative labels can confuse both parsers and quick human readers.
  3. Reverse chronological order. Put the most recent relevant experience first unless the employer explicitly asks for a functional format.
  4. Consistent dates. Use the same month/year format throughout and resolve unexplained gaps or overlaps.
  5. Clear job titles. Keep the employer's actual title. Add a short explanatory phrase only when it helps a reader understand a non-standard title.
  6. Evidence-led bullets. Start with an action, name the scope and include a verified result where one exists. Avoid a list of duties with no outcome.
  7. Honest keywords. Use the posting's language only where it truthfully describes your work, tools or domain.
  8. Readable structure. Prefer one column with clear headings. Tables, text boxes and unusual layouts may extract poorly in some systems.
  9. Contact details. Include a professional email and relevant location or work arrangement. Remove unnecessary personal identifiers.
  10. Skills that are actually used. Keep the list useful and specific; do not create a wall of tools you cannot discuss.
  11. Credentials and dates. Verify institution names, certification dates and spelling. Never imply a completed credential that is still in progress.
  12. File format. Follow the application instructions first. If there is no instruction, a simple PDF is often the safest presentation, but some employers require DOCX.
  13. File name. Use a professional name such as First-Last-Role-Resume.pdf; avoid final_final or personal information.
  14. Human read-through. Read the exported file on desktop and mobile, check every link, and ask whether each claim can survive an interview question.

How to use keywords without stuffing

Start with the job description and mark four kinds of language: core responsibilities, named tools, domain knowledge and preferred qualifications. Then map each item to one of three states: proven, adjacent or missing. A proven skill can be stated directly. An adjacent skill needs an honest explanation of how it transfers. A missing skill should not be turned into a claim.

A before-and-after bullet

Before: "Responsible for improving the customer support process and reporting to management."

After: "Rebuilt the weekly support triage process for a five-person team, reducing duplicate tickets by 18% and giving managers a single view of unresolved issues."

The second version is stronger because it contains scope, action and a verifiable result. It does not need a keyword repeated three times. If you do not know the percentage, replace it with a factual statement rather than inventing a number.

Formatting checks that matter

  • Use a standard font at a readable size and keep margins consistent.
  • Make headings visually distinct but avoid relying on color alone.
  • Keep bullets short enough to scan; avoid paragraphs inside bullets.
  • Use a simple hyphen or standard bullet character, not an image or icon.
  • Check that the PDF text is selectable and the document is not a scan.
  • Do not include a photo, date of birth or marital status unless the local application explicitly requires it.

Final five-minute review

  1. Read the job title and first sentence. Do they match the role?
  2. Check every number, date and tool name against the source.
  3. Search for repeated keywords; replace repetition with evidence.
  4. Open the exported file and confirm the page count and links.
  5. Save a local copy and record where the application was submitted.
