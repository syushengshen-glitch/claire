A discovery-call script that exposes budget, deadline, and decision-maker

Ask better questions, disqualify bad fits early, and leave the call with a clear next step.

Reviewed October 12, 2026 · Practical operations guide · Advertising is labeled separately

Quick answer: Use a structured call sheet: current situation, desired outcome, consequence of doing nothing, constraints, budget range, decision process, and next step. Ask, listen, and write down exact words.

The problem

A friendly call can end with enthusiasm but no budget, no deadline, and no decision process. That is not a sales problem; it is a discovery problem.

Open with the outcome of the call

Explain that the goal is to understand whether you are a fit, not to pressure anyone. Ask permission to take notes and to ask a few direct questions. This reduces defensiveness and makes budget and timeline questions feel normal rather than intrusive.

Map the current situation and the cost of waiting

Ask what happens today, who is affected, and what they have already tried. Then ask what happens if the problem stays unresolved for another quarter. A real cost of waiting can be lost revenue, wasted staff time, missed deadlines, compliance risk, or a stalled launch.

Ask for a range, not a number

Instead of asking “What is your budget?”, ask “What range have you set aside for this, and what would make the upper end worthwhile?” If they have no range, ask what similar work has cost before and who approves the spend. A prospect who refuses every budget question is a high-risk proposal.

Identify the decision process

Ask who else needs to agree, what evidence they need, and what happens after the call. If the person on the call cannot approve the work, ask to include the decision-maker before writing a detailed proposal. This single question prevents most unpaid proposal work.

Close with one specific next step

End with a summary in their words, the decision date, and the smallest next step: a written scope, a paid discovery session, a sample, or a second call with the approver. Send the summary the same day so the conversation does not drift.

Example

Call summary email

Hi Sam — thanks for the call. What I heard: the current intake form loses about 20% of requests, the new site must launch before the October campaign, and Maya approves the final budget. You asked for a fixed-scope proposal with two payment milestones. I will send a one-page scope by Thursday and include the two open questions we listed.

Next step: review the scope by Monday, then decide whether to book the build week.

Checklist

  • Did I learn the current situation in their words?
  • Did I ask what happens if nothing changes?
  • Do I know the budget range and who approves it?
  • Is the deadline tied to a real event?
  • Did I confirm one specific next step and date?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Pitching for most of the call instead of asking questions.
  • Writing a detailed proposal before confirming budget and authority.
  • Accepting “sometime next quarter” as a deadline.
  • Ending without a written summary and a decision date.

FAQ

How long should a discovery call be?

Twenty to thirty minutes is enough if you have a structured sheet. Longer calls usually mean the scope or decision process is unclear.

What if the prospect refuses to discuss budget?

Offer a paid discovery step or a narrow range of options. If there is still no budget signal or decision process, decline the proposal politely.

Should I record the call?

Only with consent and a clear reason. A written summary is usually safer, easier to correct, and sufficient for a small project.
