Contract review checklist: 12 red flags before you sign

A plain-language checklist for scope, payment, ownership, termination, and liability.

Reviewed October 12, 2026 · Practical operations guide · Advertising is labeled separately

Quick answer: Check the scope, payment trigger, revision limit, ownership, confidentiality, termination, liability, and dispute terms before signing. If a clause is unclear, ask for plain language.

The problem

Small projects are often won with a short proposal and a friendly email. That is fine until a payment, ownership, or termination dispute appears.

Scope and acceptance

The contract should define the deliverable, the acceptance criteria, the revision limit, and how changes are approved. “To the client’s satisfaction” is not an acceptance criterion; it is an unlimited obligation. Replace it with a review period and a specific approval step.

Payment and suspension

Look for the deposit, milestone triggers, due dates, accepted payment methods, late fees, and what happens if the client delays feedback or payment. You need the right to pause work when an invoice is overdue without being in breach of contract.

Ownership and portfolio rights

Clarify when ownership transfers, what happens to drafts and source files, and whether you can show the work in a portfolio. Do not assign all intellectual property before final payment without a clear reason.

Termination and liability

Check the notice period, payment for work completed, kill fee, and what happens to third-party costs. Review whether liability is capped and whether indemnity clauses are mutual or one-sided. This is not legal advice; use a qualified professional for high-risk contracts.

Ask for the change before signing

List the unclear clauses in writing, propose plain-language alternatives, and keep the response. A client who refuses every reasonable clarification is showing you how the project will go.

Example

Plain-language alternative

Instead of: “Contractor assigns all work product upon creation.”

Use: “Ownership transfers to Client when the final invoice is paid. Contractor retains the right to display the work in a portfolio unless a written confidentiality agreement says otherwise.”

Checklist

  • Is the deliverable and acceptance step specific?
  • Are revisions limited and defined?
  • Are payment triggers and late-payment rights clear?
  • Does ownership transfer only after payment?
  • Is termination payment addressed?
  • Is liability capped and understandable?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Signing a template without reading the termination and ownership clauses.
  • Accepting “satisfaction” or “reasonable” as the only acceptance standard.
  • Allowing unlimited changes without a change process.
  • Assuming a friendly email chain will protect you later.

FAQ

Can I use a free contract template?

A template can be a starting point, but it must match your jurisdiction, project risk, and business structure. For high-value or regulated work, have a professional review it.

What if the client will not sign a contract?

Use a written proposal with the same scope and payment terms, and decide whether the risk is acceptable. For significant projects, a signed agreement is a reasonable requirement.

Should I include a kill fee?

A kill fee compensates for reserved capacity and work completed if the client cancels early. The amount should match your actual risk, not a punitive number.
