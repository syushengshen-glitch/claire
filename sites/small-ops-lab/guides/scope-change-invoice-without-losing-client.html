How to invoice a scope change without losing the client

Turn an awkward request into a documented decision with a clear price and timeline.

Reviewed October 12, 2026 · Practical operations guide · Advertising is labeled separately

Quick answer: Acknowledge the request, restate the original scope, describe the impact, offer two options, and ask for a written decision before work continues.

The problem

Scope changes often arrive as casual requests: “Can we also add…?” If the request is absorbed, the project margin disappears. If it is refused bluntly, the relationship suffers.

Respond before you start the extra work

When a new request arrives, thank the client for the idea and say you will check the impact on scope, timeline, and cost. This small pause prevents you from doing unpaid work while you decide how to price it.

Show the original boundary

Quote the relevant line from the signed scope or quote. This is not an accusation; it is a reminder. “The current scope includes two revision rounds and five pages; this request adds a new page template and a content migration.”

Quantify the impact in client language

Translate the change into additional hours, a new deadline, a dependency, or a risk. If the client cares about launch date, say what the change does to launch date. If they care about budget, show the additional cost.

Offer two real options

Option A: add the change with a price and a new deadline. Option B: keep the original scope and log the request for a phase two. A third option can be a smaller version of the request that fits the current budget. Avoid an open-ended “we will figure it out later.”

Get approval in writing and update the invoice

Ask the client to reply with the chosen option and the approval. Then issue a change order or an updated invoice before the work starts. If the client delays the decision, keep the original timeline and scope.

Example

Change-order email

Thanks for the request. The current quote covers five pages and two revision rounds. Adding a sixth page with a booking form requires about six extra hours and moves the launch by three business days. Option A: add it for $900 and move the launch to October 24. Option B: keep the current scope and plan the booking page for phase two. Reply with A or B and I will update the schedule.

Checklist

  • Did I pause before doing the extra work?
  • Did I quote the original scope accurately?
  • Did I describe the impact in time, cost, and risk?
  • Did I offer a real alternative?
  • Do I have written approval before starting?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Absorbing the change to avoid conflict.
  • Saying no without offering an alternative.
  • Giving a verbal estimate that the client treats as final.
  • Starting the work before the change order is approved.

FAQ

What if the change is small?

Small changes still consume time. Use a light change log for minor items and a formal change order when the cost or timeline impact is material.

Can I bundle changes into one invoice?

Yes, if each change is itemized and approved. A bundled invoice without descriptions makes future disputes harder to resolve.

What if the client refuses to pay for the change?

Do not start the work. Keep the original scope and document the decision. If the relationship is otherwise good, offer a future phase instead of an unpaid exception.
