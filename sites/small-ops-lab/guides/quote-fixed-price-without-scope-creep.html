How to quote a fixed-price project without absorbing unlimited revisions

A repeatable quoting method that prices discovery, revisions, assumptions, and change control.

Reviewed October 12, 2026 · Practical operations guide · Advertising is labeled separately

Quick answer: Quote the smallest complete outcome, list what is included and excluded, cap revisions, state the client responsibilities, and price any new request through a change order.

The problem

A fixed price is not dangerous by itself. The danger is quoting before the scope, revision limit, assumptions, and change process are defined.

Define the finished outcome before pricing

Write one sentence that describes what will exist when the project is done: a five-page site, a 30-second video, a monthly report, a working booking flow. If the outcome needs three adjectives and two commas, it is not defined enough to quote.

Separate discovery from production

Charge for the work that reduces uncertainty: stakeholder interviews, content audit, technical review, or a small prototype. A paid discovery step produces a better quote and prevents the client from treating your estimate as a free research project.

Cap revisions in the quote, not in a late email

State the number of revision rounds, what counts as a revision, and how long the client has to respond. A revision changes the current approved direction; a new requirement, new audience, or new deliverable is a change request.

Price assumptions and client responsibilities

List what the client must provide and by when: copy, images, access, feedback, legal review, or a decision-maker. If those inputs arrive late, state how the timeline and price are affected. This turns a future argument into a documented assumption.

Use a change order with a range and a decision

When scope changes, pause work, describe the request, estimate the additional time and cost, and offer two options: add the change, or keep the original scope and log the request. Do not continue working and hope to negotiate later.

Example

Scope boundary language

Included: five responsive pages, one content-editing session, two revision rounds, and a launch checklist.

Not included: copywriting, photography, paid ads, third-party subscription fees, and new page templates.

Client responsibilities: approve the sitemap within three business days and provide final copy before the build week.

Any request that changes the page count, audience, or approval path will be estimated separately before work starts.

Checklist

  • Can I describe the finished outcome in one sentence?
  • Have I listed what is excluded as clearly as what is included?
  • Is the revision limit and response time written down?
  • Are client responsibilities tied to dates?
  • Does every new request go through a change order?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Quoting a broad category instead of a finished deliverable.
  • Leaving revisions unlimited because the client seems reasonable.
  • Absorbing a change to avoid an awkward conversation.
  • Forgetting third-party costs, taxes, travel, or subscription fees.

FAQ

Is fixed-price work always riskier than hourly?

No. Fixed price works when scope, assumptions, and change control are explicit. Hourly work can also become risky if the client has no decision process.

How many revision rounds should I include?

Two rounds is a common starting point for small projects. The important part is defining what a round includes and what happens next.

What if the client refuses change orders?

That is a scope-risk signal. Either narrow the original promise, move to a time-and-materials agreement, or decline the project.
