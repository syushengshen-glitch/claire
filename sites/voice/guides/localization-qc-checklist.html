Dubbing & Localization

Localization QC checklist for dubbed and subtitled audio

Check meaning, names, numbers, timing, lip sync, cultural context, and disclosure before release.

Reviewed October 28, 2026 · Practical operations guide · Advertising is labeled separately

Quick answer: Run separate reviews for source meaning, target-language naturalness, performance, timing, technical audio, on-screen text, and disclosure. Use a native reviewer who has the source and context.

The problem

A translated script can be grammatically correct and still fail: a name is pronounced differently, a number is read incorrectly, a joke does not travel, or the voice no longer fits the character.

Review meaning before style

Compare the target script with the source, not only with the previous translation. Check omissions, added claims, changed uncertainty, and lost caveats. A smoother sentence that changes the meaning is still a defect.

Check names, numbers, and units

Listen to every name, acronym, date, price, unit, and legal phrase in context. Confirm whether the target market uses the same currency, date format, or measurement system. Numbers are a common source of serious errors.

Review performance and lip sync

Listen for unnatural pauses, misplaced emphasis, emotion that does not match the scene, and mouth movement that clearly conflicts with the audio. Perfect lip sync is not always possible, but the performance should not fight the picture.

Check cultural and visual context

Review idioms, gestures, images, flags, gestures, and examples. A line can be translated correctly and still be culturally confusing. Ask a native reviewer who knows the target audience, not just the language.

Verify technical audio and disclosure

Check levels, clipping, sibilance, music balance, subtitle timing, and synthetic-voice disclosure. The final QC should happen in the playback environment and format the audience will receive.

Example

QC defect log

00:42 — price read as 15 dollars; source says 50.

01:18 — product name stress differs from approved guide.

02:03 — joke literalized; reviewer suggests cultural equivalent.

03:11 — subtitle remains on screen after the speaker changes.

Checklist

  • Did a native reviewer compare source and target meaning?
  • Are all names, numbers, units, and legal lines checked?
  • Does the performance fit the scene and timing?
  • Are visual and cultural references appropriate?
  • Are audio, captions, and disclosure checked in the final format?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Reviewing only the target language for grammar.
  • Letting one reviewer handle language, performance, and technical QC alone.
  • Ignoring on-screen text and visual context.
  • Testing only on headphones instead of the target device.

FAQ

Who should approve the translation?

A native target-language reviewer with access to the source and context should approve meaning. The client or subject expert should approve product and legal claims.

Do I need lip sync for every language?

Not always. Decide based on format and audience. Dubbing can prioritize natural performance over exact mouth shapes, but the trade-off should be intentional.

How many QC passes?

At least two: a meaning and performance review, then a technical and final-format review. High-risk content may need a third subject-matter review.
