Synthetic voice disclosure: when and how to tell the audience

A practical disclosure workflow for ads, training, podcasts, social video, and customer-facing audio.

Reviewed October 28, 2026 · Practical operations guide · Advertising is labeled separately

Quick answer: Disclose when the audience could reasonably be misled about whether a person is speaking, when platform rules require it, or when the content could affect trust or safety. Use plain language in the audio, on-screen text, and description.

The problem

Audiences may assume a voice is human, especially when the performance is convincing. In some contexts that assumption creates deception, even when the voice rights are valid.

Identify the deception risk

A synthetic narrator reading a generic product description has a different risk from a cloned founder endorsing a financial product. Ask whether the audience would change their interpretation if they knew the voice was synthetic. If yes, disclosure is likely needed.

Use more than one channel

Place the disclosure where the audience will encounter it: spoken at the start or end, on-screen text, video description, podcast notes, and metadata when the platform supports it. One hidden line in a footer is not meaningful disclosure.

Write plain-language disclosure

Say what is synthetic and what is not. “This narration uses a synthetic voice” is clearer than “AI-enabled media experience.” If a real person’s likeness or voice is involved, say so accurately and only with permission.

Check platform and market rules

Advertising platforms, social networks, app stores, broadcasters, and local regulators may have specific disclosure formats. Rules can change, so verify the current requirement before publishing.

Keep a disclosure record

Save the final wording, placement, date, platform, and approval. If the content is edited or republished, confirm the disclosure still travels with it. Disclosure is part of the media asset, not a one-time email.

Example

Disclosure examples

Audio: “This narration was created with a synthetic voice.”

On-screen: “Synthetic voice · Human-reviewed script”

Description: “This video uses an AI-generated narrator. Product claims were reviewed by the brand team.”

Checklist

  • Could the audience be misled about whether a person is speaking?
  • Does the platform require a specific disclosure?
  • Is the disclosure in the audio, visual, and description?
  • Is the wording plain and accurate?
  • Is the disclosure recorded with the final asset?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Hiding disclosure in a footer or metadata only.
  • Using vague terms that do not say the voice is synthetic.
  • Assuming a cloned voice is automatically disclosed by the platform.
  • Removing disclosure when republishing a clip.

FAQ

Does every AI voice need disclosure?

Not always. The requirement depends on the context, deception risk, platform, and law. If the audience could reasonably think a real person is speaking, disclose.

Should the disclosure be at the start?

For high-risk content, put it where the audience will see or hear it before acting. For low-risk entertainment, an on-screen label and description may be enough, but verify platform rules.

Does disclosure replace consent?

No. Disclosure explains the synthetic nature; consent addresses the rights of any real voice or likeness used.
