How to choose a voice for brand narration

A casting matrix that separates audience, emotion, authority, pace, and accent from personal taste.

Reviewed October 28, 2026 · Practical operations guide · Advertising is labeled separately

Quick answer: Define the listening context, the emotional job, the trust level, the pace, and the accent requirement before listening to demos. Score candidates on those criteria, then test the best two in the actual edit.

The problem

Voice selection often starts with a subjective favorite. The result can sound polished but wrong for the audience, the product, or the place where the audio will be heard.

Write the listening brief before the audition

State who hears the audio, where they hear it, how long they listen, and what they should do next. A voice for a 20-second social ad is not the same as a voice for a 40-minute training module. Include the device, background noise, and whether the listener can replay the audio.

Separate warmth from authority

Warmth signals closeness and reassurance; authority signals expertise and control. A voice can have both, but most performances lean one way. Decide which one the message needs first, because a warm performance can make a technical claim sound less credible while an authoritative performance can make a friendly product sound distant.

Test accent and pronunciation against the real audience

Accent is not a decoration. It affects comprehension, trust, and whether the listener feels the message is for them. Use the actual brand names, product names, numbers, and regional terms in the audition. Do not ask a voice actor to fake a dialect they do not own unless the brief and ethical review justify it.

Score the voice in context, not in isolation

Put the shortlisted voices over the real music, ambience, and visuals. Listen on a phone speaker, headphones, and a laptop. Score comprehension, emotional fit, pacing, consistency, pronunciation, and the amount of editing required. A beautiful isolated demo can fail once it sits under a busy soundtrack.

Document the decision and the alternate

Record why the chosen voice fits and which alternate is acceptable. Note the required tone, pace, pronunciation, accent, and usage rights. A clear casting record makes future episodes, translations, and pickups much easier.

Example

Casting matrix fields

Audience: operations managers listening on a laptop during work.

Emotional job: calm confidence, not hype.

Pace: measured, with clear pauses around numbers.

Trust cues: plain language, no exaggerated emphasis.

Test words: product name, three numbers, one acronym, one regional term.

Checklist

  • Is the listening context described?
  • Did I choose warmth or authority as the primary job?
  • Does the audition use real names and numbers?
  • Did I listen in the final playback environment?
  • Is the alternate and decision recorded?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing a voice because it sounds impressive in a demo.
  • Ignoring accent and pronunciation until the final edit.
  • Using a fake dialect for authenticity.
  • Scoring voices without music, ambience, or the final visuals.

FAQ

Should the brand owner’s voice be used?

Only if it fits the job and the owner is comfortable. Founder voice can build trust, but it may not scale across languages or long series.

How many voices should I audition?

Three to five shortlisted voices are enough for most projects. More options often produce decision fatigue rather than a better fit.

Can AI voices be used for brand narration?

Yes, when the voice rights, disclosure, quality, and platform rules are handled. Label synthetic media when required and never clone a person without permission.
