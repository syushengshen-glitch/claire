Accent and representation checklist for voice casting

Use accent as a casting requirement, not a costume, and document the reason for every choice.

Reviewed October 28, 2026 · Practical operations guide · Advertising is labeled separately

Quick answer: Define the accent requirement, ask whether the voice authentically belongs to the community, test comprehension with the intended audience, and avoid asking performers to fake a dialect for decoration.

The problem

A voice can unintentionally signal a stereotype, misrepresent a region, or make a community sound like a caricature. The problem is rarely one accent; it is an undisclosed casting decision made without context.

Ask why the accent is needed

An accent may support comprehension, local relevance, character identity, or brand recognition. Write the reason. If the only reason is “it sounds more authentic,” the brief is not specific enough to guide a responsible choice.

Prefer lived experience when identity matters

If the script represents a specific region or community, a performer with that lived experience is usually better positioned to make the language sound natural. This is not a universal rule, but it should be the default when authenticity is part of the promise.

Test comprehension with the actual audience

Play the audition for people who resemble the intended listener, not only the internal team. Ask what they understood, what felt forced, and whether any word carried an unintended meaning. Record the answers instead of relying on a single opinion.

Check the script for stereotypes

Read the script without the voice. Are regional references, idioms, or character descriptions doing real work, or are they performing an identity? Remove anything that treats a community as a punchline or a decorative style.

Document the casting rationale

Record the accent, dialect, language variety, performer background when relevant, audience test, and approval. This protects the project from accidental misrepresentation and makes future localization decisions consistent.

Example

Representation note

Accent requirement: Taiwanese Mandarin for a local customer-support module.

Reason: the audience is in Taiwan and the script includes local service terms.

Test: five target users listened on phone speakers and confirmed the pronunciation of three product names.

Constraint: no exaggerated regional stereotype; the performance should sound like a normal workplace explanation.

Checklist

  • Is the reason for the accent written down?
  • Does the performer fit the language variety?
  • Did target listeners test comprehension?
  • Did we remove decorative stereotypes?
  • Is the casting rationale recorded?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Casting an accent only because it sounds “international.”
  • Asking one performer to imitate several unrelated dialects.
  • Testing with the internal team only.
  • Treating a community as a style reference rather than an audience.

FAQ

Is it ever appropriate to use an accent outside the performer’s background?

Acting can involve accent work, but the decision should be transparent, respectful, and appropriate to the context. For commercial narration where identity is the point, lived experience is usually safer.

Should I include accent labels in the brief?

Use precise language such as language variety, region, and reference recordings. Avoid vague labels that conflate nationality, ethnicity, and accent.

How do I handle mixed audiences?

Choose the primary audience and test an alternate. You may need separate recordings rather than one voice trying to represent everyone.
